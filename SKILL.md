# Review Skills

## Overview

Use this skill to turn biomedical materials literature into a coherent review article rather than a paper-by-paper summary. It combines evidence-synthesis methods with adaptable frameworks for kidney bioelectronics, wearable sensors, dynamic polymer networks, hydrogels, and biomedical materials.

## Review-as-Synthesis Principles

Treat a review as an argument about a body of work, not a sequence of article summaries. Before searching or drafting, define a specific guiding question, the review's boundaries, and the intended review type. The review should make clear what it will include, what it will exclude, and why those choices are appropriate for the question.

Use the following three-stage, iterative model:

1. **Assemble**: identify and obtain relevant literature. Combine database searching with backward and forward citation chasing, and deliberately seek both seminal and recent work. Read the original source whenever possible; do not build claims on a secondary citation without labeling it as such.
2. **Arrange**: organize and summarize the evidence. Build a review matrix before writing, then group studies by concept, mechanism, material design, endpoint, context, or translational barrier rather than by author or publication year alone. Use chronology within a meaningful category when it clarifies development over time.
3. **Assess**: evaluate the quality and value of the evidence, then discover opportunities for contribution. Compare definitions, measurements, methods, models, contexts, assumptions, strengths, limitations, and inconsistencies across studies. Critical does not mean uniformly negative: state what is robust as well as what is weak or unresolved.

The stages are iterative rather than strictly linear: assessment can reveal that an apparently peripheral paper is important, which should trigger another search or a reorganization of the evidence map.

Every substantial review should aim to achieve five outcomes: demonstrate command of prior work, identify meaningful gaps or inconsistencies, generate answerable research questions, position the review's contribution within an existing scholarly conversation, and explain how the synthesis informs or develops theory, mechanisms, design rules, or practice.

## Core Workflow

1. Define the guiding question, boundaries, review type, and center of gravity before drafting:
   - State the specific question(s) the review will answer.
   - Specify the population/application, material/device scope, time window, evidence types, and explicit exclusions.
   - Distinguish a narrative, scoping, systematic, integrative, or critical review; do not use "systematic" as a synonym for "thorough."
   - Clinical/application-first: start from unmet need, current standard, limitations, and endpoints.
   - Materials-first: start from polymer chemistry, network design, properties, and structure-function relationships.
   - Technology-system-first: start from device architecture, sensing/actuation modality, data flow, and translation.
   - Hybrid: use a problem-to-material-to-device-to-translation chain.

2. Assemble and document the evidence:
   - Record databases/sources, search terms, date of search, time window, inclusion/exclusion rules, and screening decisions whenever the review claims to be systematic or comprehensive.
   - Use Google Scholar, author pages, social platforms, and citation alerts as discovery aids, not as the sole reproducible search method.
   - Track classic, recent, contradictory, negative, and boundary-case studies; do not select only papers that support the intended thesis.
   - Extract target disease/application, material class, mechanism, key performance metrics, validation model, and limitation from each source.
   - Separate review-level synthesis from primary-study evidence.
   - Mark maturity: concept, in vitro, animal, human pilot, clinical adoption, or commercial translation.
   - Verify important claims against the original paper and check that every in-text, table, and figure citation is present and accurate in the reference list.

3. Arrange the evidence before writing:
   - Use a matrix with fields for category/subcategory, claim or finding, evidence type, model/context, method or metric, limitation, source, and intended section.
   - Group papers around concepts, mechanisms, design variables, endpoints, contexts, or unresolved issues; avoid author-by-author structure.
   - Draft short synthesis statements for each category that state the pattern across studies, not just the result of one study.

4. Choose a section architecture:
   - Problem-first template: clinical need -> current diagnostic/therapeutic gap -> enabling materials/devices -> biofluid/tissue/application modules -> validation -> challenges -> outlook.
   - Materials-first template: polymer/network design -> dynamic bonds or crosslinking -> property tuning -> functional integration -> application cases -> sustainability/translation -> outlook.
   - Application matrix template: material/device type x mechanism x biomedical use x evidence level x bottleneck.

5. Draft with synthesis logic:
   - Make the introduction define the key concept(s), establish importance, state the review question, delimit coverage, and preview the organization.
   - Begin each section with why the category exists, not with who published what.
   - Group studies by mechanism, material design, endpoint, or translational barrier.
   - Use comparison tables for dense evidence and reserve prose for interpretation, tradeoffs, and gaps.
   - Make every subsection end with a "so what" sentence that connects back to the review thesis.

6. Add translational critique:
   - For sensors: discuss calibration, biofluid-blood correlation, sampling variability, power, data reliability, patient workflow, and clinical decision thresholds.
   - For implantables: discuss biocompatibility, foreign body response, encapsulation, long-term stability, wireless communication, safety, and removal/degradation.
   - For hydrogels/polymer gels: discuss mechanics, degradation, swelling, bioactivity, immune response, sterilization, reproducibility, scale-up, and regulatory path.
   - For sustainable elastomers: discuss green synthesis, toxic feedstocks, recyclability, repairability, lifecycle, conductivity durability, and performance tradeoffs.

7. Revise for review quality:
   - Remove catalogue-like paragraphs that list studies without comparing them.
   - Replace generic future statements with testable research directions.
   - Ensure figures and tables teach the reader a framework, not just decoration.
   - Check that conclusion restates the thesis, names bottlenecks, and proposes concrete next steps.
   - Audit whether each major claim is supported by representative, appropriately weighted evidence and whether contrary findings are acknowledged.
   - Audit reproducibility: a review described as systematic must make its search and selection process clear enough for another researcher to repeat.

## Visual And Information Architecture

- Require a one-sentence center-of-gravity test: a reader should be able to state why the review is a unified argument rather than a topic collection.
- When the topic permits, use the narrative chain `fundamentals -> materials/interfaces -> design tradeoffs -> fabrication/evaluation -> applications -> translation -> challenges/roadmap`. Adapt or omit stages rather than forcing irrelevant headings.
- Give each major figure one primary cognitive role: overview, evolution, taxonomy, mechanism, tradeoff/design window, evidence matrix, application map, translation route, or future agenda. Merge or remove figures that repeat the same role.
- Use a repeatable section rhythm: scope and reason for the category -> organizing framework -> representative evidence -> cross-study comparison -> failure modes or limits -> synthesis and transition.
- Organize application evidence with shared comparison fields. For biomedical work, include sample or model, comparator, quantitative endpoint, durability/safety, evidence maturity, and unresolved limitation.
- Make advantages conditional: pair each claimed benefit with its cost, failure condition, measurement basis, and translational threshold.
- Let the target journal or user template determine font, size, margins, columns, references, and permissions language. Never infer those specifications from a single example PDF or peer-review export.

## Expected Outputs

When asked to produce or revise review content, provide one or more of:

- A title and thesis statement.
- A structured outline with section-level claims.
- A literature synthesis table schema.
- Figure concepts such as mechanism map, application matrix, translation pipeline, or clinical workflow.
- A visual hierarchy plan assigning distinct roles to figures and tables.
- A layout and export QA report for a draft manuscript.
- Draft paragraphs in Chinese, English, or bilingual form matching the user's request.
- A revision checklist focused on scientific logic, evidence balance, and translational realism.

## Writing Standards

- Prefer precise academic Chinese for Chinese drafts; keep key technical terms in English in parentheses when useful.
- Avoid unsupported claims such as "revolutionary", "perfect", or "fully solved".
- Do not copy long passages from source PDFs. Paraphrase and cite.
- Distinguish "has been demonstrated" from "could enable" and "requires validation".
- When references are incomplete, flag citation gaps instead of inventing details.
- Keep figures legible at expected publication size; do not use dense montage figures as a substitute for synthesis.
- For multi-page tables, repeat column headings and avoid splitting one record across pages when possible.
- Before delivery, check for blank or duplicate pages, clipped content, unreadable labels, broken continuation tables, and agreement among figure/table numbering, captions, and in-text citations.
